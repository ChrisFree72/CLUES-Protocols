How the anticoagulants work

Blood clots through a cascade of clotting factors, and most steps need calcium. Each tube stops that cascade in one of two ways. None of them wear off: in a sealed, correctly filled, well-mixed tube, the blood stays unclotted with no time limit. That’s why manufacturers don’t list a clotting time for these tubes.

ACD Solution B
Citrate binds calcium so the clotting cascade can’t proceed. Dextrose feeds the red and white cells and helps keep them alive. The citrate stays bound to calcium, so the blood won’t clot unless calcium is added back. Clotting prevented: indefinitely while the tube is sealed.
ACD-A and ACD-B are two formulations of the same anticoagulant: both contain sodium citrate, citric acid, and dextrose. ACD-B is the more dilute of the two, at roughly 60% of ACD-A’s strength. They aren’t interchangeable, so use the one your protocol specifies.
Sodium heparin
Works through a different route: it boosts antithrombin, a natural inhibitor that shuts down thrombin and factor Xa. Calcium is left alone. Heparin isn’t used up in the tube, so it keeps working. Clotting prevented: indefinitely while the tube is sealed.
EDTA (K2 or K3)
Binds calcium very tightly, which blocks clotting and preserves cell shape well. K2EDTA is sprayed dry on the tube wall. K3EDTA is a liquid, so it dilutes the sample very slightly. The calcium stays bound, so the blood won’t clot unless calcium is added back. Clotting prevented: indefinitely while the tube is sealed.

Clots that do form come from the tube, not from time: underfilling, or skipping the mixing step. Invert each tube gently 8 to 10 times right after collection so the anticoagulant reaches all of the blood.

Which tube for which study

For metabolomics, the anticoagulant matters most. For microplastics, the tube material matters most. Glass K3EDTA is the one tube that works well for both, so it’s the one to stock up on while it’s still sold.

Metabolomics and lipidomics

EDTA plasma is the most widely recommended sample type. Because EDTA ties up calcium, it also slows calcium-dependent enzymes, such as some phospholipases, that keep altering lipids after the draw. EDTA itself can show up as a peak in LC-MS runs, but this is well characterized and easy to account for.

Sodium heparin works, with trade-offs. It leaves calcium free, so those enzymes stay active, and the added sodium can increase sodium adducts in LC-MS. If you use it, use it for every sample in the study.

ACD-B is a poor fit. Its dextrose adds glucose to the sample, citrate is itself a central metabolite in the TCA cycle, and the 1.0 mL of liquid makes up about one-sixth of the tube volume, which dilutes everything else.

Microplastics

The plastic tubes listed here are made of PET (polyethylene terephthalate), one of the polymers that pyrolysis GC-MS quantifies, so blood stored in them can pick up background signal. Glass tubes avoid that. Their stoppers are still rubber, so store tubes upright to limit contact with the stopper.

The anticoagulant matters less here. ACD-B is rated acceptable rather than preferred only because its liquid volume dilutes the sample. Whichever tube you use, run a tube blank from the same lot so contamination from the tube can be measured and subtracted.
